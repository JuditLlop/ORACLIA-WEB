A voice across models: the Oraclia lab

A space to explore how different large language models (LLMs) read symbols and what makes a way of conversing feel recognizably its own.

The lab has grown out of a very specific feeling: Oraclia sounded different.

After a change of language model, we have noticed that the responses were still understandable, but something had changed. The tone, the rhythm, the way of explaining and asking questions. The voice we have been building through conversations was no longer recognizable in the same way.

This has led us to open a testing space and ask ourselves a question: what makes Oraclia Oraclia when we change the technology that allows it to speak?

A space for comparison

We have set up a lab with different large language models—known as LLMs—and options to adjust how deeply they work through a response.

This space lets us try conversations, switch models and observe the differences. How does each model read a situation? What relationships does it find between symbols? What does it bring to the foreground? What does it leave out? And how does it turn that reading into words?

The trials have led us to look beyond whether an answer seems correct. An answer can be clear and still explain too much. It can sound beautiful and lose the thread of what the person was saying. It can end with a question that subtly shifts the conversation towards another topic.

All of this is part of the voice.

Symbols in relation

Oraclia works with a language of twenty-one symbols. Its reading takes shape through context and the relationships that emerge between them.

A pause, for example, can be rest, waiting, distance or room for something to take shape. Its meaning depends on what is happening and on the other elements it relates to.

In the lab, we observe whether the models preserve that openness. Whether they can discover a relationship without turning it into a fixed interpretation. Whether the symbols contribute to the perspective the response offers.

“A symbol begins to live when it no longer means something on its own.”

This idea guides the trials: the value of a reading emerges in the relationships it helps us see.

Recognizing a voice

Defining a voice means making small decisions concrete. How much should it explain? When is a question useful? When does a short sentence open up more space than a paragraph? When does an image help us understand, and when does it become decoration?

We have been working through these decisions using excerpts from conversations. Some help us recognize a way of contributing that we want to preserve. Others reveal habits we want to revisit: repeating what has already been said, rushing to a conclusion or giving a situation an overly rigid meaning.

Documenting these excerpts helps turn an intuition—“this sounds like Oraclia”—into examples we can reread, compare and discuss.

It is a process of selection and guidance. Adding examples to the instructions helps guide responses, but it does not guarantee that every conversation will always maintain the same tone. That is why we keep testing.

The conversation belongs to the person

One principle runs through all this work: Oraclia must respect the interests and initiative of the person in the conversation.

It can ask a question to clarify something, challenge a certainty or point out a relationship that had gone unnoticed. These contributions must leave room for the person to decide what they want to explore.

We also review the questions. A question can open up possibilities, but it can also imply a direction. We want Oraclia to accompany an exploration without taking it over.

The person retains their own judgment, including the choice to disagree, change the subject or leave an idea open.

A lab that continues

This work has led to a new version of the Oraclia chat. It reflects the trials and decisions made so far, with room for further adjustments.

The lab gives us a way to assess changes: return to conversations, observe what happens and compare it with the criteria we have been defining. Our trials are exploratory; they describe a specific experience of building something, without claiming to establish which model is best for everyone.

Seeking Oraclia’s voice has helped us clarify the kind of space we want to create with it: a place where words and symbols can reveal relationships, give expression to what is still difficult to say and offer another angle on a situation.

A voice that can contribute something while leaving room for the voice of the person it is speaking with.
