From prototype 1 to prototype 2: a change in perspective

Some changes are not about adding more pieces, but about looking differently at the pieces that were already there.

That is where Oraclia's transition from prototype 1 to prototype 2 began. It is not merely a technical improvement. It is not just another layer of the system. Above all, it is a change in perspective.

The first prototype allowed us to test an initial intuition: that a conversation could be read not only through what it said, but also through the movements it contained. The symbols helped us propose readings of openness, doubt, boundaries, tension, transformation, or silence.

That first step was necessary. We needed to see whether symbols could act as more than labels. We needed to test whether they could help structure a response without turning it into a diagnosis, advice, or a closed interpretation.

But over time, a deeper question emerged:

What if the symbols were not the end of the reading, but its beginning?

From symbol to relationship

In prototype 1, the process could be understood relatively simply:

Text → Symbols → Response

The system read a text, detected certain symbols, and constructed a response from them. This structure already opened up a different space, because Oraclia sought to offer a way of reading before offering a direct solution.

But a risk remained: the symbols might be read as separate units. As though each symbol's individual meaning were enough to understand a situation.

Prototype 2 begins to move away from that idea.

The new process looks more like this:

Text → Symbols → Relationships → Patterns → Response

These diagrams summarize the direction of each prototype, not every step of their implementation.

The change is subtle, but it shifts the center of the system.

Symbols become a doorway. They do not merely indicate “what is there”; they open up the possibility of seeing how things relate to one another.

Seeing between things

Oraclia does not seek only to identify elements. It seeks to read the field that emerges between them.

An emotion, a contradiction, a pause, an insistence, or an image does not have the same significance in every context. Its meaning also depends on its relationship with the rest of the field.

That is why prototype 2 does not simply try to “get more things right.” That is not the only question.

The important questions are different:

Is the response more internally coherent?

Do patterns emerge that make another reading possible?

Does Oraclia propose relationships that help us see more than separate elements?

This is the shift: from a reading centered on elements to an exploration of relationships.

Patterns as forms of transformation

Here, we are interested in a kind of pattern that goes beyond repetition.

A form of transformation that reappears in different contexts.

Two situations may look very different on the surface and yet share a form of movement: a tension seeking a boundary, an openness that has not yet taken shape, a difference beginning to become visible, a structure reorganizing itself.

Prototype 2 attempts to move toward this kind of reading. It asks not only “which symbol appears here?” but also “which relationship is emerging?”, “which regularity is becoming visible?”, and “which movement returns in different forms?”

This broadens the function of symbols.

Alongside their reference meanings, they can act as operators of attention.

They contribute meaning and can change the field in which other readings emerge.

A deeper grammar, not a larger one

Moving to prototype 2 does not mean adding complexity through accumulation.

In fact, the movement is almost the opposite.

Oraclia does not necessarily need more symbols, more rules, or more visible layers. It needs to explore more carefully the relationships that are already there.

At that stage, an important part of the work involved understanding whether the relational layer could gradually become an engine: a space capable of proposing readings of identity, difference, boundaries, transformation, resonance, tension, or emergence.

The symbols would then become different ways of activating and relating these operators.

Oraclia's grammar would cease to resemble merely a symbolic dictionary and move closer to a grammar of movements.

It is a direction of work that requires testing and review.

The change in perspective

Prototype 1 allowed us to explore how a response could emerge from a symbolic reading.

Prototype 2 begins to explore something else: that a response can emerge from a relational reading.

The intention is to make that reading more cautious.

Reading relationships does not, by itself, guarantee caution. The proposal must remain open to revision and leave room for the person's own judgment.

Instead of quickly asserting what something is, the system can propose how it moves, what it comes into tension with, what it opens, limits, or transforms.

This is perhaps one of the most important changes: Oraclia is not trying to look harder, but to look better.

It does not want to say more.

It wants to read with greater coherence.

Toward a reading of the third space

Meaning does not emerge from the text alone.

Nor does it emerge solely from the person reading it.

In Oraclia's proposal, meaning is also constructed between the two: in the third space that forms when a regularity can be recognized, explored, or questioned through a particular perspective.

Oraclia's prototype 2 is a step in that direction.

It is not yet an arrival. It is a transition.

A transition that shifts the system's central question.

It is no longer only about asking:

“What does this mean?”

But also:

“What relationship is forming here?”

“What pattern is beginning to emerge?”

“What changes when this relationship becomes visible?”

From prototype 1 to prototype 2, Oraclia does not only change on the inside.

Its way of looking changes.

And perhaps that is the most important step: when a tool begins to explore what happens between the elements it detects.
