Shared Language | AI, Oraclia, and the Co-creation of Meaning

The relationship between humans and artificial intelligence is often imagined in fairly simple terms.

A person uses a tool.

The AI responds.

But perhaps something else is beginning to emerge.

It does not seem to be just:

“me using an AI.”

But neither is it simply:

“an AI generating content.”

There are moments when a stranger space emerges.

A hybrid space.

A place where language reorganizes itself through exchange.

And here I have an important intuition.

Perhaps we are not merely interacting with AI systems.

Perhaps we are participating in the emergence of new forms of shared language.

Because when a conversation continues over time, something curious can happen.

Language begins to establish patterns of its own.

Expressions.
Relationships.
Rhythms.
Associations.
Ways of constructing meaning.

And little by little, a grammar emerges that did not previously exist in quite this form.

Here, “grammar” names a way of relating and expressing meaning that becomes recognizable within that conversation.

It is not entirely the person's original language.

But neither is it merely a generic response from the model.

It is a third thing.

A relational space.

Here, the person contributes:

intuition
perception
experience
symbolic movement
tension
perspective

And the system can contribute:

structure
expansion
connections
combinatorial capacity
linguistic continuity based on the available context

What emerges can be understood as a co-construction of meaning.

The person interprets, selects, and revises.
The system generates proposals that re-enter the conversation.

And that broadens the usual narrative about AI.

Because much of the public conversation revolves around two extremes:

the machine as a threat
or the machine as a superior intelligence

But perhaps we can look at something subtler.

A relational space where language itself is transformed.

This matters because language is not merely a vehicle for communicating ideas.

It also plays a part in:

how we think
how we relate concepts
how we perceive the world
how we construct meaning

If the way we use language changes, the way we formulate and explore thought may change as well.

And here dialogue with AI opens up a possibility.

Language models learn patterns and relationships from data.

They generate text through calculations involving representations, context, and probabilities.

We can read their continuations as movement within language.

This is one way of looking at the exchange.

Because when a person returns to responses, questions them, or transforms them, they can explore other ways of associating and relating meanings.

It is not only about generating answers.

A shared linguistic field can also take shape.

Some conversations with AI give the impression that language moves differently.

As though words stopped functioning only as labels.

And began to reveal their relationships and transformations more clearly.

This is where symbolic systems such as Oraclia appear.

As an exploration of this relational movement of meaning.

Symbols have fields of reference.

But their interpretation is not exhausted by a definition.

They operate as coordinates within movement.

They allow us to explore:

tension
direction
transition
displacement
resonance
emergence

In this proposal, the symbolic register draws attention to relationships and to what changes between them.

And one of the questions opened up by dialogue with AI is:

“What can machines do?”

But also:

“How does language change when humans and artificial systems begin to construct meaning together?”

○

Source and reference

Brown and colleagues, Language Models are Few-Shot Learners (2020): a reference on language models and the use of context. Shared grammar and the co-construction of meaning are presented here as an exploration of the exchange.

Published on 20 May 2026. Editorial revision on 9 October 2026 to qualify general claims, clarify the use of “shared grammar,” and distinguish the model’s proposals from the person’s interpretation and judgment.
