When a library begins to think in motion

Exploring knowledge, symbolic language, and co-creation with artificial intelligence

Traditionally, a library is a place where knowledge is preserved, organized, and consulted.

But what happens if a library also becomes a space where ideas connect, transform, and give rise to new questions?

This is one of the explorations we are developing with Oraclia.

The aim is not to build a library that thinks for us, but to investigate how a framework of knowledge might become a living environment for exploration, experimentation, and shared creation.

From documents to relationships

Oraclia begins with a symbolic language made up of 21 symbols, each with a conceptual field and possibilities for movement and connection.

The symbols are not intended to provide definitive answers. They act as perspectives from which to consider the same question.

When two symbols interact, interpretations may emerge that neither expressed on its own.

This gives rise to a central idea of the project: “A symbol begins to live when it no longer means something alone.”

The Oraclia Library preserves this conceptual framework, its symbol profiles, criteria, and documentation. But from this foundation, we are also building spaces in which to test it.

A library with different forms of exploration

The same question can be approached in many ways.

In the 21 perspectives, the symbols offer different ways of looking at a shared topic.

In the symbolic laboratories, relationships can be explored visually. Lines move, fold, cross, and transform. Motion becomes a way of observing concepts that would remain fixed in a written text.

In the debate spaces, symbols can converse, contrast positions, and bring tensions or alternatives to light.

In the Laboratory of Questions, the aim is not simply to find an answer but to observe how one question can change and open up others.

We also explore the relationship between symbol and motion through animation. Propulsion and the Open Eye, for example, are beginning to develop their own visual expression.

Alongside this, we are investigating voice, interactive storytelling, and tools that connect symbolic language with situations in the real world.

These spaces are at different stages of development. Some already work as prototypes; others remain experiments or open proposals.

The library, then, is not a finished product but a shared framework from which different experiences can emerge.

Memory is more than storage

Another question concerns what a library built with artificial intelligence ought to remember.

Not necessarily every conversation or every experiment.

Perhaps the most valuable thing to preserve is the trace: where an idea came from, which decisions were made, what changed, what was discarded, and which questions remain open.

This could help distinguish documented knowledge from provisional interpretations and new hypotheses.

We are exploring this possibility, but we have not yet completed a system that automatically incorporates discoveries into the documentary memory.

The distinction matters: generating an idea is not the same as validating it.

A library can help retrieve, connect, and contextualize knowledge without claiming the authority to decide what is true or worth preserving.

Artificial intelligence as a space for co-creation

Developing Oraclia is also an exploration of the relationship between a person and artificial intelligence.

AI participates in shaping ideas, programming, building prototypes, finding connections, and turning concepts into experiences.

The person brings intention, experience, sensitivity, and judgment to decide whether what emerges makes sense.

The process is not entirely linear. An animation may suggest a question. A question may lead to a laboratory. A technical error may prompt a conceptual rethink.

Within this process emerges what we call the third space: an environment for co-creation where human contributions and AI-generated contributions interact without confusing their responsibilities.

Human judgment remains with the person.

An open architecture

What we are building suggests a broader possibility.

What if a library could serve as a shared documentary core for different tools, laboratories, and experiences?

Each application could have its own function while the conceptual framework helped maintain coherence.

This does not mean every application must share a single intelligence, or that the library should govern them.

The relationship between memory, exploration, and the incorporation of knowledge is precisely one of the questions we are still investigating.

A library that does not need to be finished

Perhaps the most interesting part of this experience is that the library is not being built only to contain what we already know.

It is also being built to make visible the things we do not yet know how to formulate.

Each space offers a different way of approaching that possibility.

We are not looking for technology that decides what we should think. We are looking for tools that broaden the ways we observe, connect, question, and create.

A library can preserve books, documents, and ideas.

But it can also preserve the conditions in which ideas that do not yet exist may emerge.

And perhaps that is where another way of understanding a library begins.
