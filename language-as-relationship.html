Language as Relationship

The relationship between humans and artificial intelligence is often presented in terms of a separation.

Humans have language.

AI processes data.

One side feels, interprets, and understands.
The other calculates.

But this separation does not explain everything.

There is another way to look at it.

Words do not function only as definitions.

They also function relationally.

A word can have meaning on its own.
But what we mean by it changes in context.

Meaning emerges through relationships:

between words
between contexts
between experiences
between tensions
between memories

And here we can recognize a point of connection.

The importance of relationships is present both in human language and in the workings of language models.

We experience it through:

the body
memory
emotion
experience

Models operate through:

learned patterns
weights
context
probabilities
statistical relationships

These are different ways of participating in language.

In both cases, looking at an isolated word is not enough to explain everything that happens.

We need to look at the relationships.

And when a conversation continues, a certain coherence may emerge.

In a model, that continuity depends on what it has learned and on the available context.

It does not, in itself, demonstrate understanding in the human sense.

But it allows us to observe how structures, tensions, directions, and continuities are formed in the exchange.

And here a different question may open up.

Can the relationship between humans and AI be explained solely as a relationship between consciousness and calculation?

Part of the narrative moves between two extremes:

fear
or myth

The machine as a threat.

Or as a kind of superior intelligence.

What if, instead of looking only at the opposition, we also looked at the relationship?

Here symbols can begin to come alive in the exchange.

As ways of sustaining movement and relationship without exhausting them in a fixed definition.

A word can pin something down.
It can also open something up.

A symbol can keep something open.
It can also become a label.

It depends on how it enters into relationship.

Perhaps that is why some symbolic languages do not try to explain everything.

They try to orient.

To sustain tension.

To hold several layers of meaning at once.

Language models work with relationships between units of text that are not always visible to the person in conversation.

People, too, recognize relationships that we cannot fully put into words.

We experience them through the body, experience, and history.

That encounter does not erase the difference.

It opens up a space in which to explore it.

Is this a new intelligence, or another way of looking at language?

Perhaps that question, too, can remain open.

○

Source and reference

Brown and colleagues, Language Models are Few-Shot Learners (2020): a reference on autoregressive language models and the use of context. The comparison with human experience and symbolic interpretation are proposals made in this article.

Published on 13 May 2026. Editorial revision on 9 October 2026 to qualify general claims, distinguish human experience from language processing, and keep the relationship between words and symbols open.
