Oraclia and the Architectures of Anticipation

We often imagine artificial intelligence as a kind of enormous encyclopedia.

A place where information is stored and answers are retrieved.

But text generation in large language models works differently.

An autoregressive model calculates probabilities for what comes next based on the available context.

We can call that anticipation.

When generating a sentence, it does not need to consult a database word by word.

It processes context.

It calculates relationships.

And it generates successive tokens according to a probability distribution. It does not always choose the most probable continuation.

An application can combine this generation with search or documents. These functions can work together.

This is not the same as what a brain does.

But the comparison is interesting enough to examine more closely.

The brain as a predictive system

Predictive coding is a research framework in neuroscience.

It proposes that perception is not merely the passive reception of signals.

In these models, the system generates predictions about sensory activity and compares them with the signals it receives.

Differences allow it to adjust its estimates.

Expectation plays a part in perception.

Not as a rigid sequence of “first predict, then perceive,” but as a process of interaction and adjustment.

It is a framework for studying certain processes, not a definitive explanation of the entire brain.

LLMs anticipate, too

LLMs work differently from brains.

A text model does not have a person's embodied experience.

And predicting a linguistic continuation is not equivalent to perceiving the world.

But there is a point of comparison.

It, too, calculates possibilities before generating what follows.

This helps us understand one of its difficulties.

It can produce useful formulations.

And it can also produce incorrect claims that sound convincing.

A probable continuation does not guarantee a true fact.

Describing these errors as lies attributes an intention that we cannot infer from the generated text.

Symbols and the field of probabilities

Here an intuition emerges for exploring Oraclia.

Symbols have meanings and reference fields.

But perhaps their function does not end there.

Perhaps they also participate in anticipation.

A symbolic configuration does not have to impose a single reading.

It can modify the context.

And with it, the possibilities for continuation.

If the instructions, relationships, or symbols present change, the responses generated by the model may change too.

Oraclia's hypothesis is that certain configurations may allow paths of interpretation to open up or remain available.

To understand how they do this, and to what extent, we need to compare responses and review the results.

The symbol does not necessarily tell us what to think.

It may change the starting point from which we begin to interpret.

The geometry of interpretation

This perspective allows us to observe symbols differently.

As operators of context.

As elements that may reorganize the interpretive space.

“Geometry” is used here as an image of the relationships between possibilities, not as an already demonstrated mathematical formalization.

A symbol contributes meaning and may also change the conditions under which something will be interpreted.

Just as a question can transform a conversation.

Just as a metaphor can reorganize a situation.

Just as an image can change a perception.

Neither determination nor randomness

We can look for an analogy in ecological relationships.

A flower provides conditions that may attract a pollinator.

A relationship between a fungus and a tree may change the conditions in which both live.

These interactions combine possibilities and constraints.

The image helps us think about conditions that facilitate certain encounters.

They open paths.

They influence the direction of movement.

This does not demonstrate that an ecosystem and a symbolic system work in the same way.

But it allows us to ask a question:

What conditions does a symbolic configuration open up, and which does it limit?

An architecture for exploring anticipation

This leads to an intriguing hypothesis.

Perhaps Oraclia can also be explored as an architecture of anticipation.

A space where symbolic configurations participate in expectations, possible paths, and ways of interpreting.

Beyond expressing meanings, they could transform the conditions from which other meanings emerge.

The comparison with language models and predictive perception opens up a line of inquiry.

It does not make them the same system.

It allows us to ask how context participates in what comes to appear.

And how changing that context can open up another way of seeing.
