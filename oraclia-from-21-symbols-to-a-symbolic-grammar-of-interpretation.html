Oraclia: from 21 symbols to a symbolic grammar of interpretation

Oraclia continues to change.

Not because we are adding more answers, more symbols or more rules, but because we are becoming better at distinguishing what each part of the system should do.

Over recent months, the prototype has been moving beyond an overly simple idea: that a sentence can be reduced to a symbol or a specific interpretation.

Its current direction is different.

Oraclia attempts to read relationships, forms and transformations.

Symbols are not words

Oraclia works with an alphabet of 21 symbols.

But those symbols do not function as a dictionary of automatic translations.

∞, ?, ∆, ≈ and ○ have reference meanings, but no fixed translation that can be applied mechanically to any conversation.

We also understand them as operators.

A symbol can change what becomes visible, which difference emerges, which relationship gains importance, or how a situation is reorganized.

That is why one of the project's central ideas remains:

Symbols do not exhaust meaning. They can change the field in which meaning emerges.

From symbols to configurations

A conversation can activate several symbolic fields at once.

What matters is not only which ones appear, but what happens between them.

They may coexist, come into tension, reinforce one another, separate, reorganize or alter the entire configuration.

This has brought a clearer architecture into view within Oraclia.

The architecture we are exploring begins with a symbolic reading. Relationships are then observed. A minimal form is built from those relationships. And when a new conversational turn arrives, that form can be compared with the previous one.

Oraclia therefore does not observe only what is present.

It also seeks to observe what continues, what disappears, what appears and what changes its relationships.

An absence can still have an effect

This shift has brought a particularly interesting question into focus.

If something stops appearing explicitly in a conversation, that does not necessarily mean it has stopped having an effect.

An absence may remain connected to elements that are still present.

But Oraclia must not automatically conclude that this absence “weighs on”, “blocks” or “determines” anything.

It can preserve the earlier relationship as a revisable hypothesis and observe whether later language provides grounds for continuing to consider it.

The distinction may seem small, but it matters:

not present does not necessarily mean not active.

What if there is not enough structure to interpret?

This is where one of the prototype's more recent proposals emerged: the null symbolic field.

A null field does not mean there is nothing there.

Nor does it mean that there is no weight, relationship, emotion or movement.

It means something much more specific:

Oraclia does not have sufficient grounds to build a well-supported symbolic configuration without inventing one.

Faced with this gap, a system might fill in the context with a likely interpretation.

Oraclia tries to do the opposite.

If it does not know, it can preserve that not-knowing.

And if the conversation needs to continue, it can ask for a specific distinction.

For example, if someone says:

“I have a meeting tomorrow and I don't want to go.”

There is a clear position, but we do not know the reason.

It could be lack of interest, tiredness, discomfort, disagreement or something else entirely.

Rather than choosing a cause, Oraclia can ask:

“What makes you not want to go?”

An open question can introduce a distinction without assuming an interpretation.

Null is not infinity

This idea has also required us to separate two things that seemed similar.

A null field is not the symbol ∞.

Null describes the state of the reading: there is not yet enough of a configuration.

∞, by contrast, has its own symbolic field. In this design, it can guide an operation: keeping possibility open and asking for differentiation.

This separation aims to prevent memory from attributing to a person something they have not expressed.

If there are three consecutive turns without sufficient configuration, the trace should not be:

∞ → ∞ → ∞

but rather a persistence of the null field.

That does not mean “openness”, “blockage” or any psychological state.

It means only that, over several consecutive moments, there was not enough structure to support a reading.

Memory must not turn trajectories into identities

As we explore how to preserve forms and compare them over time, another risk emerges.

Recognizing a recurring trajectory is not the same as defining a person.

The fact that a configuration has appeared several times allows us to say only:

this trajectory has appeared before.

It does not allow us to claim:

this is what this person is like.

Memory must preserve possible continuities, but each new situation must be able to disprove them.

The person takes precedence over the model Oraclia constructs.

A reading must also be open to correction

This idea guides the system's governance.

When someone corrects an interpretation made by Oraclia, that correction must not be treated as an objection the system needs to overcome.

It is new information.

If Oraclia interprets tension and the person says there is no tension, that reading loses its priority.

The system should not try to defend it, rephrase it or immediately replace it with another theory.

It must revise its model.

The reading is provisional.

Describe before interpreting

Another rule that has become established as a design criterion is seemingly simple:

first describe the observable movement; then, if there are sufficient grounds, propose an interpretation.

A decision is not necessarily rigidity.

Withdrawal is not necessarily fear.

A boundary is not necessarily protection.

Change is not necessarily openness.

Perhaps they are, in a particular case.

But the conversation must support that interpretation.

The pipeline is changing, too

Internally, Oraclia is being organized into more distinct layers.

In the design we are exploring, the reader detects and proposes. The relationships layer organizes connections. Form represents the configuration. Dynamics compares different moments. Governance decides what the system may do with that reading. And the composer turns it back into human language.

This description reflects the direction of the prototype's development at that time, not a guarantee that every layer has been implemented or validated.

The separation serves an important purpose:

the final language should not have to carry all the system's intelligence.

The answer could be simple because much of the work would have happened beforehand.

Questions remain open

The system is still a prototype.

At this stage, we continue working on how to represent forms that the current grammar cannot describe, how to govern different ways of deciding without imposing one, and how to build memory without turning recurrences into identities.

There is also work left to do in separating the internal layers more clearly and progressively simplifying the composer.

But perhaps the most important change is not technical.

It is that we have adopted, as a design criterion, a possibility that is often difficult for both systems and people:

being able not to know yet.

Not immediately filling the gap.

Not turning a possibility into a certainty.

Not confusing a reading with reality.

And allowing the difference to emerge before giving it form.
